If you are a startup founder or a small business owner, this is usually the first question you ask — and the hardest one to get a straight answer to. One agency quotes $500, another quotes $25,000, and both say they build "professional websites". So what is the real price?

The short answer: a small business website costs from almost nothing (if you build it yourself) to tens of thousands of dollars (for a large custom build). Most professionally built small business websites in 2026 fall between $1,500 and $8,000 with a freelancer or small studio, and $5,000 to $25,000+ with an agency — plus hosting, domain and maintenance every year.

But the sticker price is never the whole price. This guide breaks down what you actually pay for, what cheap quotes leave out, and how to compare quotes fairly — so you can budget with confidence and hire the right team.

What the price actually includes

A website is not one thing. When you pay for a professionally built site, you are paying for several parts. Ask every provider to itemise them, so you can compare like with like:

  1. Design (UI/UX design): How the site looks, how it guides visitors, and how it works on a phone. Good UI/UX design is not decoration — it is what turns a visitor into an enquiry.
  2. Development: Building the pages, forms, and features so they load fast and work on every device. This is custom website development when the site is built for your business, not a template with your logo pasted in.
  3. Content: The words on the page. Many quotes exclude copywriting — then the site launches with empty pages because nobody wrote the text.
  4. Domain and hosting: Your web address (about $12 a year) and the server the site lives on. Ask whose name the domain and hosting are in — the answer should be yours.
  5. Basic SEO setup: Clean headings, proper page structure, meta titles and descriptions, mobile-first layout, and Google Search Console. This is what makes Google able to find and rank the site.
  6. Testing (QA): Checking the site on phones, tablets and desktops, testing the contact form, and fixing bugs before launch.
  7. Handover and training: A short session so you can update the site yourself after launch — and the source code, design files and logins in your own accounts.

If a quote is far cheaper than the rest, one of these is usually missing. The most common missing pieces are content, SEO, testing and after-launch support.

DIY vs freelancer vs agency — which route fits your budget?

DIY builders: $0–$500 upfront, $16–$50 a month

Builders like Wix and Squarespace are the cheapest way to get online. You pick a template, add your text and photos, and publish. The trade-off is your time: a good-looking site still takes many hours, and SEO, speed and conversion are mostly on you. A DIY site is a fair choice for a simple presence — a one-page site with your services, phone number and opening hours.

Freelancer: $800–$8,000

A skilled freelancer can be perfect for a clearly defined project with a tight budget. You talk directly to the person doing the work, and the cost is usually lower than an agency. The risks are capacity and continuity: one person can fall ill, disappear, or have no backup, and a freelancer rarely covers design, development, SEO and support all at once. Small agency: $5,000–$25,000

An agency suits you better when the project needs several skills at once — design, development, SEO and support — or when you expect the website to grow. You get a team, a structured process, and someone to call after launch. It usually costs more. A web development agency in Pakistan like PrimeLoft can often deliver agency quality at a lower cost than a US, UK or Australian agency, because the team is based in Dera Ghazi Khan, Pakistan, and works with clients worldwide.

There is no universal right answer. Decide by the project, not by the label. Compare written proposals, check live work, and pick the provider whose answers were the clearest.

WordPress vs Squarespace — which is cheaper?

This is one of the most searched comparisons, and the answer depends on what you need.

WordPress development suits most business websites because it is flexible and easy to manage. WordPress itself is free, but running it is not: a domain and shared hosting cost about $111 a year at the bare minimum, and about $735–$4,095 a year once managed hosting and someone to keep it updated are included. WordPress is the right choice when you want full control, custom features, or a site that will grow.

Squarespace development suits a simple, beautiful site that you can manage yourself. Squarespace plans run about $19–$99 a month on annual billing. It is faster to launch and easier to maintain, but less flexible for custom features. Squarespace is the right choice for a brochure site, a portfolio, or a small online store that does not need custom software.

PrimeLoft builds both. The honest rule is: choose the platform by the job, not by fashion. If you are unsure, ask for a recommendation in writing — a good agency will tell you which one fits and why, even if it is the cheaper one.

How much does an e-commerce website cost?

An online store costs more than a brochure site, because it needs product pages, a cart, payments, shipping, tax and order emails. A basic e-commerce store from a freelancer typically costs $3,000–$10,000, and a larger custom store from an agency $8,000–$25,000+. E-commerce website development also has ongoing costs: payment gateway fees on every sale, hosting, and someone to add products and fix issues.

Before you approve an e-commerce quote, check that it includes: product import, payment gateway setup, shipping rules, tax settings, order confirmation emails, and a test purchase before launch. A store that cannot take a payment on day one is not finished.

How much does a mobile app cost? How much does SEO cost per month?

Two more questions every founder asks — and the answers are ranges, not single numbers.

Mobile app development: A simple app with a few screens costs far less than a full native app with accounts, payments and notifications. As a planning guide, a basic app starts in the low thousands, and a full native mobile app is a multi-thousand-dollar project that typically takes 6–12 weeks. If you only need bookings, a quote form, or a product catalogue, a mobile-friendly website may do the same job for far less. Ask the agency to justify the app before you pay for one.

SEO services: SEO is not a one-time cost. A small business typically pays $50–$500+ a month for ongoing SEO, depending on the market and the work involved. Be careful of anyone who promises "page 1 in 30 days" for a flat fee — SEO is a slow, compounding investment. What you should expect every month is clear reporting: which keywords moved, which pages gained traffic, and what was done. PrimeLoft's SEO services include technical SEO auditing, on-page keyword work, Core Web Vitals optimisation and targeted off-page work — and honest reporting of verified numbers only.

How long does it take to build a website?

Timeline is the second most searched question after cost, and the honest answer is: it depends on scope, but most projects fit a pattern.

  • Custom marketing sites, WordPress builds and Squarespace launches: typically 2 to 4 weeks.
  • Full-scale SaaS platforms, custom software and native mobile apps: generally 6 to 12 weeks.
  • A one-page landing site: sometimes 1 week, if the content and photos are ready on day one.

The biggest cause of delay is not development — it is content. If the text, photos and logo are ready before work starts, the project moves fast. If they are not, add two weeks. PrimeLoft works in short agile sprints with working demos at regular reviews, so you see the site taking shape instead of waiting for a big reveal at the end.

The hidden costs nobody mentions upfront

Budget for these, or they will surprise you after launch:

  • Domain renewal: about $12–$60 a year, forever.
  • Hosting renewal: $50–$500 a year for a small site, more for a busy store.
  • Business email: a separate per-user subscription, not included in hosting.
  • Maintenance: security updates, backups, and small edits. A care plan is typically $50–$350 a month. Without one, every change is billed separately.
  • Content updates: new pages, new photos, new products — budget 15–25% of the build cost per year for maintenance unless you have an included care plan.
  • Premium themes and plugins: some are licensed, not owned. Ask which parts you own and which are only licensed.

A good quote lists all of these. A bad quote hides them.

How to get a fair quote — a 10-point checklist

Before you sign anything, ask for these in writing:

  1. An itemised quote: pages, features, content, domain, hosting, SEO, revision rounds, and what is out of scope.
  2. Three to five live websites the provider built — open them yourself on your phone.
  3. The development process in plain stages: discovery, design, build, test, launch, support.
  4. A realistic timeline with milestones, and what happens if it slips.
  5. Who will actually do the work — the person you are talking to, or a subcontractor.
  6. Who owns the domain, hosting, content and source code (see below).
  7. What happens after launch: bug fixes, how fast, and what a change costs.
  8. How you will know it is working: enquiries, quote requests, sales — and how often you will see the numbers.
  9. A staging link before launch, so you can test the form yourself and approve the site before it goes live.
  10. A written agreement. A verbal promise or a chat message is hard to rely on later.

Who owns the code, domain and hosting?

This is the most important question in the whole guide — and the one most buyers forget to ask.

The right answer is: you do, 100%. Your domain, hosting, analytics and repository should be in accounts in your name from day one, with the developer added as a user. On milestone settlement, all source code, design systems, Figma artboards, databases and architectural assets should be transferred directly to your own Git repositories and cloud accounts. Zero vendor lock-in, ever.

If the answer is "we hold the domain for you" or "the code stays with us", walk away. If you do not own your assets, it does not matter how cheap the site was — you cannot switch providers without rebuilding it.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a small business website cost in 2026?

Most small businesses spend between $500 and $15,000 for an initial build, plus $0–$500 a month for hosting, maintenance and marketing tools. DIY builders start around $16–$50 a month. Custom builds from freelancers run $1,500–$8,000; agencies commonly quote $5,000–$25,000+ depending on pages, e-commerce and integrations.

How long does it take to build a website?

Marketing sites, WordPress builds and Squarespace launches typically take 2 to 4 weeks. Full-scale SaaS platforms, custom software and native mobile apps generally take 6 to 12 weeks.

Is a cheap website a false economy?

Often, yes. A $500 site that does not bring enquiries, cannot be found on Google, or has to be rebuilt in a year costs more than a $3,000 site that works. Judge a website by the enquiries and sales it brings, not only by the build price.

Freelancer vs agency — which is cheaper?

A freelancer is usually cheaper upfront. An agency is usually better value when the project needs design, development, SEO and support together, and when you want someone accountable after launch. Compare written quotes on the same scope — pages, features, content, SEO and support — not only the bottom line.

Who owns my website when it is finished?

You should, 100% — domain, hosting, content, source code and design files, in accounts in your name. Confirm it in writing before you pay anything.
